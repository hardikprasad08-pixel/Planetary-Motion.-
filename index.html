From Newton's law to the Euler–Lagrange equation

Every boxed result below is exactly what drives the simulation on the left — nothing is hand-waved.

1 · Physical System

The model consists of two bodies: a central mass \(M\) and an orbiting mass \(m\). We take the simplified limit

\[ M \gg m \]

so the central body can be treated as fixed at the origin (an optional two-body mode below relaxes this and lets both bodies orbit their common centre of mass). Gravitational attraction supplies the central force responsible for all orbital motion:

\[ F=\frac{GMm}{r^{2}}, \qquad \mathbf{F}=-\frac{GMm}{r^{3}}\mathbf{r} \]

The minus sign says the force vector points opposite to \(\mathbf r\) — i.e. always toward the central body, which is what keeps the orbit bound instead of flying apart.

Orbital trajectoryConic section — ellipse, parabola or hyperbola.
3 · Assumptions
  1. Both bodies are treated as point masses for gravitational calculations.
  2. The central body's mass is much greater than the orbiting body's mass.
  3. The central body is initially assumed fixed at the origin.
  4. Only gravitational interaction is considered — no other forces act.
  5. Air resistance and other external/drag forces are neglected.
  6. Newtonian gravity is used throughout (no relativistic correction in the governing equations).
  7. The primary analytical derivation assumes planar motion.
  8. The resulting orbit may be circular, elliptical, parabolic or hyperbolic depending on initial conditions.
  9. Relativistic effects are neglected in the equations that actually drive the numbers.
  10. The spacetime-fabric mesh is a conceptual, non-quantitative visualization only.
4 · Coordinate Systems

We start in Cartesian coordinates:

\[(x,y,z)\]

Because gravity is a central force, planar polar coordinates describe the motion far more naturally:

\[(r,\theta), \qquad x=r\cos\theta,\ \ y=r\sin\theta\]

For fully general 3-D motion, spherical coordinates extend this:

\[(r,\theta,\phi)\]

Cartesian is what the simulator integrates numerically (it generalizes cleanly to 3-D); polar is what makes the analytic derivation below tractable, since motion under a central force stays confined to one plane.

5 · Generalized Coordinates

For the planar orbital problem we choose:

\[ q_1=r, \qquad q_2=\theta \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \dot q_1=\dot r,\ \ \dot q_2=\dot\theta \]

\(r\) and \(\theta\) are sufficient because the motion is confined to a fixed plane (angular momentum is conserved and perpendicular to that plane) — two numbers fully locate the body at any instant.

6 · Kinetic Energy in Polar Coordinates

Differentiating \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\) and combining components gives the speed-squared:

\[ v^{2}=\dot r^{2}+r^{2}\dot\theta^{2} \]

so that \(T=\tfrac12 m v^2\) becomes

\[ \boxed{\,T=\frac12 m\left(\dot r^{2}+r^{2}\dot\theta^{2}\right)\,} \]

The first term is purely radial kinetic energy; the second is tangential — the simulation's "radial vel." and "tangential vel." readouts are exactly these two pieces.

7 · Gravitational Potential Energy
\[ \boxed{\,V(r)=-\frac{GMm}{r}\,} \]

Potential energy is chosen to vanish at \(r\to\infty\); since gravity does positive work as the body falls inward, \(V\) must be negative everywhere at finite \(r\) — it gets more negative the closer the body sits to the centre.

8 · Constructing the Lagrangian

With \(L=T-V\):

\[ \boxed{\,\mathcal{L}=\frac12 m\left(\dot r^{2}+r^{2}\dot\theta^{2}\right)+\frac{GMm}{r}\,} \]

Note the plus sign on the last term — subtracting a negative potential flips it positive. (We write the Lagrangian as \(\mathcal L\), reserving plain \(L\)/\(\ell\) for angular momentum, to avoid ambiguity.)

9 · Euler–Lagrange Derivation
\[ \boxed{\ \frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \dot q_i}\right)-\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial q_i}=0\ } \]

Radial coordinate (r)

∂L/∂ṙ
\( m\dot r \)
d/dt(·)
\( m\ddot r \)
∂L/∂r
\( mr\dot\theta^{2}-\dfrac{GMm}{r^{2}} \)
E–L eqn
\( m\ddot r - mr\dot\theta^{2}+\dfrac{GMm}{r^{2}}=0 \)
\[ \boxed{\ \ddot r - r\dot\theta^{2} = -\frac{GM}{r^{2}}\ } \]

\(\ddot r\) is radial acceleration, \(r\dot\theta^2\) is the familiar centripetal term, and the right-hand side is the inward gravitational pull per unit mass.

Angular coordinate (θ)

∂L/∂θ
\( 0 \) (θ is cyclic — doesn't appear explicitly in L)
∂L/∂θ̇
\( mr^{2}\dot\theta \)
E–L eqn
\( \dfrac{d}{dt}\left(mr^{2}\dot\theta\right)=0 \)
\[ \boxed{\ mr^{2}\dot\theta=\text{constant}\ \equiv\ \ell\ } \]

Because \(\theta\) never appears in \(\mathcal L\) itself, its conjugate momentum is conserved — this is conservation of angular momentum, and it is precisely Kepler's second law (equal areas in equal times), demonstrated interactively in the Kepler tab.

10 · Governing Equations Used for Numerical Integration

Polar form:

\[ \ddot r = r\dot\theta^{2}-\frac{GM}{r^{2}}, \qquad \ddot\theta=-\frac{2\dot r\dot\theta}{r} \]

Cartesian form (what the simulator actually integrates, since it extends cleanly to full 3-D):

\[ \ddot x=-\frac{GMx}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}},\quad \ddot y=-\frac{GMy}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}},\quad \ddot z=-\frac{GMz}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}} \]

Integrated with classical 4th-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) at a user-selectable step size — a good balance of accuracy and speed for a real-time, interactive tool. The "conservation check" graphs in the Simulation tab show how flat the energy/angular-momentum curves stay, which is the practical test of whether the step size is small enough.

11 · Effective Potential

Folding the conserved angular momentum into the radial equation gives a 1-D effective potential:

\[ \boxed{\,V_{\text{eff}}(r)=-\frac{GMm}{r}+\frac{\ell^{2}}{2mr^{2}}\,} \]

The second term is a repulsive "centrifugal barrier." The minimum of \(V_{\text{eff}}\) is exactly the radius of a stable circular orbit for that angular momentum.

12 · Orbit Classification by Specific Energy
\[ \epsilon=\frac{v^{2}}{2}-\frac{GM}{r} \]
  • \(\epsilon<0\) → bound elliptical orbit
  • \(\epsilon=0\) → parabolic escape trajectory
  • \(\epsilon>0\) → hyperbolic (unbound) trajectory
\[ v_c=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}\ \text{(circular speed)}, \qquad v_e=\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}\ \text{(escape speed)} \]

Try the "Escape trajectory" and "Hyperbolic flyby" presets in the Simulation tab to see this classification flip live.

Three Empirical Laws, One Lagrangian

Kepler's Laws

All three of Kepler's observational laws fall directly out of the Euler–Lagrange derivation above.

First Law

Planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus (a circle, parabola or hyperbola are the other possible conic sections, depending on \(\epsilon\)).

Second Law

Equal areas are swept in equal times. A planet moves fastest near perihelion and slowest near aphelion — direct consequence of \(mr^2\dot\theta=\ell=\) constant.

Third Law

\[ T^{2}\propto a^{3}, \qquad\qquad T^{2}=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{GM}a^{3} \]
